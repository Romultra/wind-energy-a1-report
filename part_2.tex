\section{Part 2: Statistical analysis of multi-year wind climate}
\label{sec:part2}

\subsection{Data Description}
\textbf{Task:} Describe the data. What has been measured, in what units, at which locations, with which instruments and with what temporal resolution?

The dataset consists of eight years of wind observations from 2018 to 2026, provided as 10-minute mean values. 
The data in the file \texttt{tenMinutesData\_2018\_2024.csv} originates from the meteorological mast at the DTU Risø Campus.

The measurements are available at specific heights, including 57 m and 70 m for wind speed, and 41 m for wind direction. For the long-term climate analysis and the Annual Energy Production (AEP) calculation of the V52 turbine, the data collected at the 70 m height is of primary interest.

\subsection{Raw Data and Gaps}
\textbf{Task:} Plot the raw data as function of time. Identify any data gaps and discuss possible reasons for these gaps.

\subsubsection{Data Cleaning}
Before plotting, outliers were removed from the dataset. All wind speed measurements exceeding 37~m/s or below 0 were replaced with NaN, as these are physically unreasonable for Denmark, removing 888 values across all wind speed channels. Additionally, any sensor channel reporting the exact same value for six or more consecutive 10-minute records was flagged as a stuck sensor and replaced with NaN. In total, 128,067 values were removed across all channels, the majority from stuck direction sensors.

\subsubsection{Data Gap Analysis}
\label{sec:q10_gaps}
Gaps were identified as any interval between consecutive timestamps exceeding the expected 10-minute resolution. A total of 950 timestamp gaps were found, ranging from 11~minutes to approximately 45~hours. Only 12 gaps exceeded 3~hours.

In addition to timestamp gaps, individual sensor channels exhibit their own periods of missing data. Figure~\ref{fig:q10_gaps} shows the per-channel data availability. The most significant outages are the sonic anemometer at 57~m (17.9\% missing, with a prolonged outage in 2019) and the cup anemometer at 70~m (14.8\% missing, with degraded availability in late 2024--2025). Note that the 57~m sonic outage affects both wind speed (SWsp\_57m) and direction (Sdir\_57m) equally, as both quantities are derived from the same instrument. The remaining channels have 7--8\% missing data.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/Q10_Global_Gaps.png}
    \caption{Data availability per sensor channel. Red segments indicate missing data periods longer than 1~hour.}
    \label{fig:q10_gaps}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/Q10_Gap_Histogram.png}
    \caption{Distribution of timestamp gap durations on a log-log scale.}
    \label{fig:q10_hist}
\end{figure}

The gap duration histogram (Figure~\ref{fig:q10_hist}) reveals two distinct regimes. The vast majority of gaps are short (10--30~minutes), consistent with transient telemetry errors or brief signal loss during wireless data transmission. A smaller population of gaps spans hours to days, pointing to more sustained causes.

Possible reasons for the observed gaps include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Telemetry errors:} The high frequency of short gaps (10--20~min) suggests intermittent signal loss in the wireless data link between the mast and the logging server.
    \item \textbf{Instrument failure:} The extended sonic outage at 57~m in 2019 and the cup anemometer degradation at 70~m in late 2024--2025 indicate localized hardware failures specific to individual sensors.
    \item \textbf{Scheduled maintenance:} Simultaneous gaps across multiple channels are consistent with planned inspections and sensor recalibration.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Seasonal Wind Roses}
\label{sec:q11}
\textbf{Task:} Using the whole time series, plot a wind rose for the winter months (Dec-Feb) and one for the summer months (Jun-Aug) discuss the directional distribution of the wind. Does it match what you would expect for Denmark?

Wind direction was taken from the sonic anemometer at 70~m (\texttt{Sdir\_70m}), and wind speed was computed as the mean of the cup and sonic anemometers at 70~m (\texttt{Wsp\_70m} and \texttt{SWsp\_70m}), using whichever sensor was available when one was missing.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/Q11_WindRoses.png}
    \caption{Seasonal wind roses at 70~m for winter (Dec--Feb) and summer (Jun--Aug), 2018--2025. Colours indicate wind speed bins; radial axis shows frequency of occurrence (\%).}
    \label{fig:q11_roses}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:q11_roses} shows that winds from the west and south-west dominate in both seasons, with this sector accounting for roughly 35--40\% of all observations. A secondary, weaker peak is visible from the south east, which likely corresponds to periods of easterly flow from the Swedish mainland and baltic sea. The directional pattern is broadly consistent with what one would expect for a site in Denmark: the country is exposed to the prevailing westerlies coming off the North Sea and the Atlantic, so westerly and south-westerly winds are the most frequent throughout the year.

The main seasonal difference is in wind speed rather than direction. Winter shows a larger proportion of higher-speed events (12--15~m/s), reflected by the longer dark-coloured bars in the west to south-west sector. Summer winds are similarly directed but weaker on average. This agrees with the general expectation that storms and strong winds are more common in winter, while summer conditions tend to be calmer.

\subsection{Histogram and Weibull Fit}
\label{sec:q12}
\textbf{Task:} Define wind bins with a spacing of 1m/s and plot a histogram of the wind speed variation based on the data from 70m height for two different years, 2018 and 2024. Fit a Weibull distribution to the observed wind speed.

The same combined 70~m wind speed series used in Section~\ref{sec:q11} was used here. Wind speeds were binned in 1~m/s intervals and the frequency of each bin expressed as a percentage of total valid records. A Weibull distribution was then fitted to each year's data by maximum likelihood estimation with the location parameter fixed at zero:

\begin{equation}
    f(u) = \frac{k}{A} \left( \frac{u}{A} \right)^{k-1} \exp\left[ -\left( \frac{u}{A} \right)^k \right]
\end{equation}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/Q12_Weibull.png}
    \caption{Wind speed histograms (1~m/s bins) and Weibull fits at 70~m for 2018 and 2024.}
    \label{fig:q12_weibull}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:q12_weibull} shows that both years follow a similar distribution, and the Weibull curve fits the observed histogram reasonably well in both cases. The fitted parameters are summarised in Table~\ref{tab:q12_weibull}.

\begin{table}[H]
    \centering
    \begin{tabular}{lrrr}
        \toprule
        Year & Shape $k$ & Scale $A$ [m/s] & Valid records \\
        \midrule
        2018 & 2.24 & 7.27 & 48\,834 \\
        2024 & 2.18 & 7.37 & 43\,746 \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
    \caption{Weibull parameters fitted by maximum likelihood for 2018 and 2024 at 70~m.}
    \label{tab:q12_weibull}
\end{table}

The shape parameter $k \approx 2.2$ in both years is consistent with typical values reported for Danish coastal sites. The scale parameter $A$ is slightly higher in 2024 (7.37~m/s vs.\ 7.27~m/s), suggesting 2024 was marginally windier on average. The 2024 dataset also contains about 5\,000 fewer valid records than 2018, which is consistent with the cup anemometer outage at 70~m observed in late 2024 (Section~\ref{sec:q10_gaps}).

\subsection{AEP for V52 Turbine}
\textbf{Task:} Calculate the AEP for the V52 turbine located at the met mast site, based on the 2018 and 2024 wind measured data. Discuss implications on the yearly variability.

The power curve of the Vestas V52 (rated power 850~kW, hub height 70~m) was modelled using a cubic relation between cut-in (4~m/s) and rated speed (14~m/s), with constant rated power up to cut-out (25~m/s):

\begin{equation}
    P(u) = \begin{cases}
        0 & u < 4~\text{m/s} \\[4pt]
        P_\text{rated}\,\dfrac{u^3 - u_\text{ci}^3}{u_r^3 - u_\text{ci}^3} & 4 \leq u < 14~\text{m/s} \\[8pt]
        P_\text{rated} & 14 \leq u \leq 25~\text{m/s} \\[4pt]
        0 & u > 25~\text{m/s}
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}

The AEP was calculated by applying this power curve to the measured wind speed frequency distribution (1~m/s bins) for each year:

\begin{equation}
    \text{AEP} = \sum_j P(u_j)\, f_j \cdot 8760~\text{h}
\end{equation}

where $f_j$ is the fraction of time the wind speed falls in bin $j$.

\end{figure}

The results in Table~\ref{tab:q13_aep} show that 2024 produced about 60~MWh (5.6\%) more energy than 2018, which is consistent with the slightly higher Weibull scale parameter found in Section~\ref{sec:q12} ($A = 7.37$ vs.\ $7.27$~m/s). The capacity factors of 14--15\% are relatively low, which is expected for a site surrounded by buildings and trees at modest hub height; a coastal or offshore location would typically yield higher values.

The difference between the two years illustrates the natural year-to-year variability of the wind resource. A single year of data can therefore over- or underestimate the long-term mean AEP, which is why multi-year averages are normally used for wind energy assessments.
